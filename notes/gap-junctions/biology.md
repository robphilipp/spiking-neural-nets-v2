In higher-order vertebrates (such as mammals and birds), neuronal gap junctions—also known as electrical synapses—are much less common than chemical synapses in the adult nervous system, though they are widespread during embryonic and early postnatal development. [1] (https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/24/12/3097/273922), [2] (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24309273/)
In the adult brain and spinal cord, they are selectively preserved in specific regions that require ultra-fast signaling or synchronized firing across large groups of neurons. 1. Cellular & Subcellular Locations

Unlike chemical synapses, which typically connect an axon terminal to a dendrite or cell body, neuronal gap junctions in higher vertebrates usually form between homologous (similar) cell types via:
• Dendrite-to-dendrite (dendrodendritic) contacts. This is the most common arrangement in the adult mammalian brain, allowing nearby neurons to synchronize their subthreshold membrane potential oscillations. [1] (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24309273/)
• Soma-to-soma (somatic) contacts. Found in tightly packed clusters of neurons, such as sensory ganglia or certain brainstem nuclei. [1] (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36125001/)
• Axon-to-axon (axoaxonic) contacts. Less common, but observed in areas like the hippocampus (e.g., between mossy fibers or pyramidal cell axons) to help generate ultra-fast network oscillations. [1] (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24309273/)

2. Major Anatomical Regions in the Adult CNS

In higher vertebrates, neuronal gap junctions (primarily composed of Connexin 36 proteins) are concentrated in several key areas: [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap_junction), [2] (https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/gap-junction)
• Inhibitory Interneuron Networks (Neocortex & Hippocampus). They extensively link GABAergic inhibitory interneurons (such as parvalbumin-positive basket cells). This electrical coupling coordinates rhythmic brain waves, particularly gamma oscillations associated with attention, memory, and sensory processing. [1] (https://uen.pressbooks.pub/expertneuro/chapter/electrical-synapses-gap-junctions/), [2] (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24309273/)
• The Retina. Gap junctions are abundant across all five major classes of retinal neurons (photoreceptors, horizontal, bipolar, amacrine, and ganglion cells). They help adjust visual sensitivity between bright (photopic) and dim (scotopic) light conditions and sharpen spatial contrast. [1] (https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(13)01342-0), [2] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap_junction)
• The Thalamus. Specifically within the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN), where gap junctions synchronize inhibitory neurons to regulate sleep spindles and sensory gating. [1] (https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/24/12/3097/273922), [2] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap_junction)
• Brainstem Nuclei. Found in areas controlling rhythmic, involuntary, or rapid motor functions:
• Inferior olivary nucleus. Coordinates timing and motor learning signals sent to the cerebellum.
• Vestibular nucleus. Enables rapid reflexes for balance and eye movement.
• Locus coeruleus & Respiratory nuclei. Synchronizes arousal states and rhythmic breathing patterns. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap_junction), [2] (https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/gap-junction)
• Olfactory Bulb. Links mitral/tufted cells and interneurons within olfactory glomeruli to synchronize odor processing. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap_junction), [2] (https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(13)01342-0)
• Spinal Cord. Connects motor neurons during development and persists in certain adult motor circuits to coordinate simultaneous muscle activation. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap_junction), [2] (https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/gap-junction)

3. Developmental Distribution

During embryonic and early postnatal development, neuronal gap junctions are widely distributed across almost all brain regions—including excitatory pyramidal neurons in the cortex—forming large coupled networks. They coordinate metabolic activity and calcium waves that guide cell migration and circuit wiring before chemical synapses fully mature. Once chemical synapses are established, most gap junctions between excitatory neurons are pruned away, leaving them predominantly in the inhibitory and sensory circuits listed above. [1] (https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/24/12/3097/273922), [2] (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24309273/)

Of the patterns you described, a hybrid loop—where gap-junction-coupled neurons both receive input from and send output back to spiking neurons—is the most common architecture in the higher vertebrate brain, particularly in circuits governing memory, attention, and motor control.
However, the sensory-to-spiking feedforward model also exists prominently in specialized sensory organs like the retina.

1. The Recurrent Loop (Most Common in Cortex, Hippocampus & Memory)

In areas critical for memory and cognition—like the hippocampus and neocortex—gap junctions do not typically link the main excitatory "spiking" neurons (pyramidal cells) to one another. Instead, they link inhibitory interneurons (such as parvalbumin-positive basket cells) into a synchronized electrical web.
These interneurons themselves are fast-spiking neurons, and they are wired into a tight feedback loop with excitatory pyramidal cells:
1. Spiking → Gap-Junction Network: Excitatory pyramidal neurons fire action potentials and send chemical signals to the network of inhibitory interneurons.
2. Synchronization via Gap Junctions: Because those interneurons are coupled by gap junctions, they share electrical current and fire their own spikes in near-perfect unison.
3. Gap-Junction Network → Spiking: The synchronized interneurons send a simultaneous wave of chemical inhibition back onto the pyramidal neurons, temporarily silencing them before they fire again together.
   Why this matters for memory: This recurrent loop generates rhythmic brain waves—specifically gamma oscillations (30–80 Hz) and sharp-wave ripples. In the hippocampus, sharp-wave ripples replay sequences of neural activity during sleep and rest, which is the core mechanism for memory consolidation.

2. The Sensory Feedforward Chain (Common in the Retina & Olfactory Bulb)

Your second scenario—non-spiking or graded-potential sensory neurons connected via gap junctions that then feed into spiking output neurons—is the classic blueprint of the vertebrate retina:
1. Coupled Sensory Layer: Photoreceptors (rods and cones), horizontal cells, and bipolar cells do not fire traditional action potentials; they use graded electrical potentials. Gap junctions link these cells laterally into sheets to pool light signals in the dark or sharpen edges in bright light.
2. Transition to Spiking: These coupled networks pass their processed signals forward to retinal ganglion cells (which are also partially coupled to each other and to amacrine cells).
3. Spiking Output: Ganglion cells convert the signal into all-or-none action potentials that travel down the optic nerve to the brain.
   (Note: A variation of this also happens in the olfactory bulb, where mitral cells are both spiking neurons and coupled via gap junctions within a glomerulus to synchronize odor detection before sending spikes to the cortex.)

3. Motor & Timing Pacemakers (Coupled Spiking Neurons)

In the brainstem (such as the inferior olive, which sends timing signals to the cerebellum for motor learning), the gap-junction-coupled neurons are themselves the primary spiking/oscillating neurons. They couple together directly so that large groups of them generate subthreshold voltage waves in unison, ensuring that when they do fire spikes, they trigger muscles or cerebellar Purkinje cells at exact, coordinated intervals.
